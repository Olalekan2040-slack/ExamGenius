TOPIC: ProbabilityIntroductionProbability is the branch of mathematics that deals with the likelihood or chance that a particular event will occur. It is measured on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), or as percentages (0% to 100%).For a complete beginner:
Probability answers questions like:  “What are the chances of getting heads when tossing a fair coin?” → 1/2 or 50%  
“What is the probability of rolling a 6 on a fair die?” → 1/6 ≈ 16.67%

This topic is very high-scoring in JAMB Mathematics – usually 4–8 questions every year.
JAMB focuses on:Experimental probability (based on actual trials, e.g., coin tosses, dice rolls)  
Theoretical probability (calculated using equally likely outcomes)  
Addition rule (mutually exclusive events)  
Multiplication rule (independent events)

World-standard learning objectives (aligned with Cambridge IGCSE, WAEC, NECO, IB MYP, and international curricula):
Candidates should be able to:Understand probability as a measure of likelihood between 0 and 1
Distinguish between experimental probability (from trials) and theoretical probability (from equally likely outcomes)
Solve simple probability problems involving single events (coin, die, cards, bag of balls)
Apply the addition rule for mutually exclusive events (P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B))
Apply the multiplication rule for independent events (P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B))
Solve combined problems involving both addition and multiplication (e.g., tossing two coins, rolling two dice)
Interpret probability results in real-life contexts (games, risk, chance events)

(Assumes all outcomes equally likely – e.g., fair coin, fair die)Key Points:Experimental probability gets closer to theoretical as number of trials increases (law of large numbers)  
JAMB often gives experimental data and asks for probability

Visual Type: table & bar chart
Visual Items:  Table showing trials and outcomes (e.g., coin tossed 100 times: 52 heads)

Example Question Pattern (simple to hard – JAMB style):Simple: A coin is tossed 100 times and lands heads 48 times. What is the experimental probability of heads?
Answer: 48/100 = 0.48 or 48%
Medium: A die is rolled 60 times. The results are: 1 appears 8 times, 2 appears 12 times, etc. Find experimental probability of rolling an even number.
Answer: Even numbers (2,4,6) total appearances → sum / 60
Hard: A bag contains red and blue balls. After drawing 50 times with replacement, 32 red balls are drawn. Estimate the probability of drawing a red ball.
Answer: 32/50 = 0.64 or 64%

Common Mistakes:Confusing experimental with theoretical (use given trials, not assumed equal chances)  
Forgetting to simplify fractions

SUBTOPIC 2: Addition and Multiplication of ProbabilitiesObjective 2: Solve problems involving addition (mutually exclusive) and multiplication (independent) of probabilitiesExplanation:Addition Rule (Mutually Exclusive Events):
Events cannot happen together (e.g., rolling a 3 or 5 on a die)
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)Multiplication Rule (Independent Events):
Events do not affect each other (e.g., tossing two coins)
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)Key Points:Mutually exclusive → no overlap (e.g., head or tail on one coin)  
Independent → one event does not change probability of another  
For dependent events (without replacement), P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B after A)

Visual Type: Venn diagram & tree diagram
Visual Items:  Venn diagram for mutually exclusive (no overlap)  
Tree diagram for two independent coin tosses (4 outcomes)

Example Question Pattern (simple to hard – JAMB style):Simple: A fair die is rolled. What is the probability of getting an odd number or a 6?
Answer: Odd = 1,3,5 (3/6), 6 = 1/6
Mutually exclusive → P = 3/6 + 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3
Medium: Two fair coins are tossed. What is the probability of getting two heads?
Answer: Independent → (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4
Hard: A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find probability both are red.
Answer: Dependent → (5/8) × (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14
Advanced (JAMB combined): A card is drawn from a standard deck of 52 cards. Find P(ace or heart).
Answer: P(ace) = 4/52, P(heart) = 13/52, P(ace and heart) = 1/52
P(ace or heart) = 4/52 + 13/52 – 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13

Common Mistakes:Adding probabilities when events are not mutually exclusive (forgetting to subtract overlap)  
Multiplying when events are dependent (forgetting to adjust second probability)
