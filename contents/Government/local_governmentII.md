**Topic 11: Local Government**  
(Part II – Political Development in Nigeria)

Local government is the third tier of Nigeria’s federal structure, designed to bring governance closer to the grassroots, deliver basic services, and promote rural/urban development. Despite constitutional recognition, it remains the weakest tier due to state dominance and chronic underperformance. This topic is highly tested in JAMB because it reveals the gap between theory (federalism with three tiers) and practice (effective two-tier system).

**JAMB Objectives**  
Candidates should be able to:  
i. Trace the evolution and structure of local government.  
ii. Identify the major problems faced by local governments.

### Subtopic a: Local Government Administration Prior to 1976

**Detailed Historical Evolution and Context**  
Before the landmark 1976 reforms, local government in Nigeria lacked uniformity, democracy, and real autonomy. It evolved through distinct phases shaped by colonial indirect rule, regional politics, and early military centralisation.

1. **Colonial Period (1914–1960) – Native Authority System**  
   - Introduced by Lord Lugard under indirect rule.  
   - British governed through existing or appointed traditional rulers (emirs in North, obas in West, warrant chiefs in East).  
   - **Northern Region**: Strong emirate system – emirs had executive powers (tax collection, native courts, order maintenance) under British Residents/District Officers.  
   - **Western Region**: Obas and chiefs had advisory roles; limited elected councils introduced in 1950s (e.g., Local Government Law 1952).  
   - **Eastern Region**: No centralized chieftaincy tradition; warrant chiefs imposed (triggered Aba Women’s Riot 1929); later county/district councils.  
   - **Common Features**: No elected councils in most areas; heavy reliance on traditional rulers; British supervision; limited functions (tax, local justice, roads).  
   - **Weaknesses**: Undemocratic, corrupt (chiefs exploited powers), regionally varied.

2. **First Republic (1960–1966)**  
   - Federalism gave regions control over local government.  
   - **Northern Region**: Retained powerful Native Authorities (emirs dominant).  
   - **Western Region**: Elected local councils (more democratic after 1952 reforms).  
   - **Eastern Region**: County and district councils (participatory).  
   - **Common Problems**: No national uniformity; corruption; political patronage; weak service delivery.

3. **Military Rule (1966–1975)**  
   - Military suspended elected councils nationwide.  
   - Sole Administrators or Caretaker Committees (military officers/civil servants) appointed.  
   - Local administration became fully centralised under state military governors.  
   - **Result**: Loss of democracy; increased inefficiency; no accountability.

**Overall Pre-1976 Features**  
- No uniform national system.  
- Traditional rulers or appointed officials dominant.  
- Limited democratic participation.  
- Poor funding and service delivery.  
- Highly regionalised and politicised.

### Subtopic b: Features of Local Government Reforms (1976, 1989)

**1976 Local Government Reforms** (Most Important – Cornerstone of Modern System)

**Background and Rationale**  
Introduced by Murtala Mohammed/Obasanjo military regime (1975–1979) through the 1976 Local Government Guidelines.  
**Preceding events**: Military observed pre-1976 weaknesses (regional variations, lack of democracy, inefficiency). Post-civil war need for national unity and grassroots development. Desire to reduce ethnic/regional tensions through uniform structure.  
**Constitutional backing**: Decree No. 36 of 1978 gave legal force; later enshrined in 1979 Constitution.

**Key Features / Structure**  
- **Uniformity Nationwide**: 301 Local Government Areas (LGAs) created (later increased to 774).  
- **Democratic Elections**: Elected chairmen and councillors (4-year term initially).  
- **Council Composition**: Executive chairman + legislative councillors.  
- **Administrative Head**: Secretary to the Local Government (career civil servant).  
- **Committees**: Standing committees (education, health, works, finance).  
- **Traditional Rulers**: Advisory role only (no executive power).

**Functions** (1976 Guidelines + 1999 Constitution Fourth Schedule)  
- Primary education and adult literacy programmes.  
- Primary healthcare, dispensaries, maternity centres.  
- Markets, motor parks, sanitation, refuse disposal, slaughterhouses.  
- Construction/maintenance of rural roads, culverts, drains.  
- Water supply (boreholes, rural water schemes).  
- Agriculture extension services, veterinary care.  
- Registration of births, deaths, marriages.  
- Collection of rates, taxes, fees, licenses.  
- Community development and recreation.

**Finance**  
- Statutory allocation from Federation Account (shared vertically with federal/states).  
- Internally generated revenue (rates, market fees, licenses).  
- Grants from state and federal governments.

**Inter-governmental Relations**  
- LGAs subordinate to states (governor approves budgets, appoints auditors).  
- State ministries of local government supervise and coordinate.

**1989 Local Government Reforms** (Babangida Transition Programme)

**Background**  
Part of Babangida’s prolonged transition to Third Republic (1987–1993).  
**Preceding events**: Desire to strengthen local autonomy, align with presidential system, and deepen grassroots democracy.

**Key Features / Changes**  
- Increased number of LGAs to 449 (later 774).  
- Introduced **presidential system** at local level: Executive chairman + elected legislature.  
- Direct statutory allocation from Federation Account to LGAs (bypassing states – later reversed).  
- State/Local Government Joint Account for revenue sharing.  
- Traditional rulers strictly advisory (no executive role).  
- More emphasis on internal revenue generation.

**1999 Constitution (Current Framework – Fourth Republic)**  
- Recognises 774 LGAs as third tier (Third Schedule).  
- Guarantees democratic elections every 4 years.  
- Joint State-Local Government Account (Section 162).  
- States control creation, funding, dissolution, and supervision of LGAs (weakens autonomy).

**Comparison: 1976 vs. 1989 vs. 1999**  
- 1976: Introduced uniformity and democracy.  
- 1989: Enhanced autonomy and presidential model (short-lived).  
- 1999: Retained structure but returned control to states.

### Subtopic c: Traditional Rulers and Local Governments

**Detailed Role and Relationship**

**Historical Role (Pre-1976)**  
- Dominant in North (emirs had executive powers: tax, justice, order).  
- Advisory/influential in West (obas) and East (limited).

**Post-1976 Reforms**  
- **Strictly Advisory Role** (1976 Guidelines, 1989 Reforms, 1999 Constitution).  
- Functions of traditional rulers today:  
  - Advise chairmen/councils on customs, traditions, community needs.  
  - Preserve culture, resolve minor disputes.  
  - Promote development (attract projects, mobilise community).  
  - Act as custodians of land and heritage.  
  - No voting rights in council.  
  - No executive, financial, or legislative powers.

**Current Reality (Fourth Republic)**  
- **Informal Influence**: Especially strong in North (emirs consulted on appointments, projects).  
  - Examples: Sultan of Sokoto, Ooni of Ife, Obi of Onitsha – respected voices.  
- **Tensions and Conflicts**:  
  - Some rulers demand more powers (e.g., executive roles).  
  - Elected chairmen often see rulers as threats to authority.  
  - Disputes over chieftaincy titles, land, and influence.  
- **Legal Position**: 1999 Constitution recognises traditional institutions but limits them to advisory roles.

### Subtopic d: Problems of Local Government Administration in Nigeria

**Exhaustive List of Major Problems**

1. **Lack of Genuine Autonomy**  
   - States control creation, funding, elections, and dissolution of LGAs.  
   - Governors dissolve elected councils and appoint caretaker committees.  
   - Joint State-Local Government Account often diverted by states.

2. **Poor and Irregular Funding**  
   - Statutory allocation from Federation Account frequently delayed or withheld.  
   - Low internally generated revenue (poor tax base, evasion, weak collection).  
   - Over-dependence on federal allocation (oil-dependent).

3. **Corruption and Mismanagement**  
   - Chairmen and councillors embezzle funds.  
   - Ghost workers, inflated contracts, diversion of allocations.  
   - Lack of transparency and accountability.

4. **Political Interference and Godfatherism**  
   - Governors and party leaders impose candidates.  
   - Caretaker committees used to reward loyalists.  
   - Elections manipulated or avoided.

5. **Weak Capacity and Inefficiency**  
   - Low education/skills of many chairmen/councillors.  
   - Poor staff training and motivation.  
   - Bureaucracy and red-tapism.

6. **Insecurity and Rural Neglect**  
   - Banditry, herdsmen clashes, kidnapping affect rural LGAs.  
   - Poor infrastructure (roads, schools, health centres).

7. **Over-Dependence on Federation Account**  
   - Little incentive for internal revenue generation.  
   - Vulnerability to oil price fluctuations.

8. **Non-Conduct of Elections**  
   - Many states avoid elections to maintain control (caretaker committees instead).

9. **Ethnic and Sectional Conflicts**  
   - Boundary disputes, indigene-settler crises.  
   - Chieftaincy tussles.

10. **Constitutional Ambiguity**  
    - 1999 Constitution recognises LGAs but gives states control (weak third tier).

**Consequences of These Problems**  
- Grassroots poverty and underdevelopment persist.  
- Rural-urban migration intensifies.  
- Federalism weakened (effectively two-tier system).  
- Loss of public trust in local governance.

**Proposed Solutions**  
- Full financial autonomy (direct allocation from Federation Account).  
- Constitutional amendment to guarantee LG elections and autonomy.  
- Strengthen internal revenue generation.  
- Anti-corruption measures and transparency.  
- Capacity building and training.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. The 1976 local government reform in Nigeria is significant because it  
   A. abolished all local governments  
   B. introduced uniform structure and democratic elections nationwide  
   C. returned full executive power to traditional rulers  
   D. made local governments completely independent of states  
   **Answer: B**

2. In the current Nigerian system (1999 Constitution), traditional rulers in local government have  
   A. full executive and financial powers  
   B. only advisory roles without voting rights in councils  
   C. the power to suspend elected chairmen  
   D. control over local government budgets  
   **Answer: B**

3. A major problem facing local governments in Nigeria is  
   A. complete financial independence from the federal government  
   B. lack of autonomy and diversion of funds by state governments  
   C. over-conduct of local government elections  
   D. absence of any statutory allocation from the Federation Account  
   **Answer: B**

**Summary & Quick Revision Tip**  
- **Pre-1976**: Native Authorities → regional variations → military appointees.  
- **1976 Reform**: Uniformity, elections, democratic foundation.  
- **1989 Reform**: Presidential model, direct funding (short-lived).  
- **1999 Constitution**: Recognised but state-controlled.  
- **Problems**: State interference, poor funding, corruption, weak capacity.

Mnemonic for reforms timeline: **Pre-1976 Native → 1976 Uniform & Democratic → 1989 Presidential & Direct → 1999 State Control**.

Key phrase: **“Local governments remain the weakest tier in Nigeria’s federalism due to excessive state control and lack of genuine autonomy.”**
