**Topic 8: Principles of Democratic Government**

This topic covers the essential building blocks that make a government truly democratic. These principles distinguish genuine democracy from other systems (e.g., autocracy, oligarchy) and prevent abuse of power. In JAMB, questions frequently ask you to identify a principle, explain how it operates, give Nigerian examples, or evaluate its application (e.g., “To what extent is the rule of law practised in Nigeria?”). Mastery here is crucial because these principles reappear in topics on Nigerian institutions, federalism, and the electoral process.

**JAMB Objectives for this Topic**  
Candidates should be able to:  
i. Identify the principles of democratic government.  
ii. Determine the application of these principles.

The subtopics (principles) are:  
Ethics and Accountability in Public Office, Separation of Power, Checks and Balances, Individual and Collective Responsibility, Constitutionalism, Rule of Law, Representative Government.

### Subtopic: Ethics and Accountability in Public Office

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Ethics in public office refers to the moral principles, integrity, honesty, transparency, and sense of duty that guide public officials in decision-making and conduct. Accountability means public officials must explain and justify their actions, face consequences for misconduct, and be answerable to the people or oversight bodies. Together, they ensure power is exercised responsibly and for the public good, not personal gain.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
Emerged from Enlightenment ideas (e.g., Locke on limited government) and anti-corruption movements. In modern democracies, strengthened by post-WWII institutions (e.g., ombudsman in Sweden 1809) and global standards (UN Convention Against Corruption 2003). In Africa, post-independence constitutions incorporated these to combat colonial-style patronage.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Integrity: Acting without corruption, nepotism, or conflict of interest.  
- Transparency: Openness in decisions, budgets, and processes (e.g., public access to information).  
- Answerability: Officials explain actions (e.g., to legislature, media, courts).  
- Responsibility: Face sanctions (suspension, impeachment, prosecution) for misconduct.  
- Mechanisms: Codes of conduct, anti-corruption agencies (e.g., ICPC, EFCC in Nigeria), asset declaration, audits, public complaints commissions.

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Reduces corruption and misuse of public funds.  
- Builds public trust and legitimacy of government.  
- Improves service delivery (officials fear exposure).  
- Encourages merit-based appointments.  
- Strengthens democracy by making leaders responsive.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Weak enforcement leads to impunity (e.g., delayed trials).  
- Political interference undermines agencies.  
- Over-bureaucratization slows governance.  
- Cultural tolerance of “gift-giving” blurs ethics.  
- Selective accountability (powerful escape punishment).

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- Global: Singapore’s strict anti-corruption ethics (Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau).  
- In Nigeria: 1999 Constitution (Fifth Schedule – Code of Conduct), EFCC/ICPC prosecutions, asset declaration by public officers, but challenges include high-profile acquittals, “cash-for-votes,” and weak political will. Recent examples: Probes into former governors, but many cases drag for years.

**Criticisms and Application**  
Critics argue Nigeria’s application is poor due to “godfatherism” and weak institutions. Application determined by: existence of laws, independent agencies, judicial independence, free press, and citizen vigilance.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. Ethics and accountability in public office primarily aim to  
   A. increase government secrecy  
   B. ensure public officials act with integrity and answer for their actions  
   C. allow unlimited personal use of public funds  
   D. eliminate all forms of oversight  
   **Answer: B**

2. In Nigeria, one mechanism for accountability is  
   A. suspension of the constitution  
   B. establishment of the EFCC and ICPC  
   C. removal of all anti-corruption laws  
   D. granting immunity to all officials  
   **Answer: B**

3. A major challenge to ethics and accountability in Nigeria is  
   A. complete independence of anti-corruption agencies  
   B. political interference and delayed prosecutions  
   C. absence of any code of conduct  
   D. over-enforcement of transparency rules  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Separation of Powers

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Separation of powers is the division of governmental authority into three independent but coordinated branches (legislature, executive, judiciary) to prevent concentration of power in one person or group. Proposed by Montesquieu (Spirit of the Laws, 1748) to avoid tyranny, it ensures each branch performs distinct functions while remaining interdependent.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
Influenced by Locke (two powers) and Montesquieu (three). Applied in US Constitution (1787) and French Revolution. In Africa, adopted post-independence to check colonial-style executive dominance.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Legislature: Makes laws (deliberative).  
- Executive: Implements laws (active).  
- Judiciary: Interprets laws (adjudicative).  
- Independence: Each branch has autonomy (e.g., separate budgets, tenure).  
- No overlap in personnel (e.g., ministers not MPs in pure separation).  
- Coordination through checks (detailed next).

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Prevents dictatorship or abuse.  
- Promotes efficiency (specialization).  
- Enhances accountability (each watches others).  
- Protects liberty through balance.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Can cause gridlock (e.g., US shutdowns).  
- In practice, executive often dominates (especially in presidential systems).  
- Overlap inevitable in complex governance.  
- Weak institutions undermine separation.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- Classic: USA (strict separation).  
- In Nigeria: 1999 Constitution (Sections 4, 5, 6) clearly separates powers. However, executive influence (e.g., budget control, appointments) weakens it. Judicial independence challenged by funding/executive interference.

**Criticisms and Application**  
Application determined by: constitutional provisions, judicial courage, legislative assertiveness, and executive restraint. In Nigeria, partial application exists but executive dominance is common.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. The doctrine of separation of powers was popularized by  
   A. Karl Marx  
   B. Montesquieu  
   C. Abraham Lincoln  
   D. Thomas Hobbes  
   **Answer: B**

2. In a system with separation of powers, the legislature primarily  
   A. implements laws  
   B. interprets laws  
   C. makes laws  
   D. appoints judges  
   **Answer: C**

3. A challenge to separation of powers in Nigeria is  
   A. complete independence of all arms  
   B. executive dominance over funding and appointments  
   C. absence of any constitutional provision  
   D. judiciary controlling the budget  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Checks and Balances

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Checks and balances are mechanisms that allow each arm of government to limit or restrain the powers of the others, ensuring no branch becomes too powerful. It complements separation of powers by making branches interdependent.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
Madison in Federalist Papers No. 51 (1788) argued ambition must counteract ambition. Embedded in US Constitution; adopted globally, including Nigeria’s 1979 and 1999 Constitutions.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Legislature checks executive: Confirmation of appointments, impeachment, oversight probes, veto override.  
- Executive checks legislature: Veto power, ordinance-making (emergency), dissolution (in parliamentary).  
- Judiciary checks both: Judicial review (declare laws/actions unconstitutional).  
- Mutual restraint: No arm can act unilaterally on major issues.

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Prevents tyranny and abuse.  
- Promotes moderation and compromise.  
- Enhances accountability.  
- Protects rights through review.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Can cause paralysis or inefficiency.  
- Strong executive may weaken checks (e.g., Nigeria).  
- Requires strong institutions and political maturity.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- USA: Congress overrides presidential veto (2/3 majority).  
- In Nigeria: Senate confirms ministers/judges; president vetoes bills; Supreme Court reviews (e.g., election petitions). Challenges: Executive non-compliance with court orders.

**Criticisms and Application**  
Application determined by institutional strength and political culture. In Nigeria, checks exist but often undermined by partisanship.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. Checks and balances ensure that  
   A. one arm of government dominates the others  
   B. each arm can limit the powers of the others  
   C. there is no separation of powers  
   D. the judiciary controls the legislature  
   **Answer: B**

2. In Nigeria, one way the legislature checks the executive is through  
   A. veto power over bills  
   B. confirmation of ministerial appointments  
   C. appointment of the Chief Justice  
   D. control of the armed forces  
   **Answer: B**

3. A limitation of checks and balances in practice is  
   A. complete elimination of executive power  
   B. potential for gridlock and inefficiency  
   C. absence of any judicial role  
   D. automatic supremacy of the legislature  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Individual and Collective Responsibility

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
- **Individual Responsibility**: Each public official (minister, permanent secretary) is personally accountable for their decisions and actions.  
- **Collective Responsibility**: The entire cabinet/government accepts responsibility for policies and decisions; if defeated, the whole cabinet resigns (especially in parliamentary systems).

**Historical Origins and Context**  
From British cabinet tradition (19th century). In presidential systems, individual responsibility is stronger; collective weaker.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Individual: Personal liability (e.g., resignation for scandal).  
- Collective: Cabinet solidarity; ministers defend government policy publicly.  
- In Nigeria: Ministers individually answerable to president; collectively to National Assembly (e.g., budget defence).

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Promotes careful decision-making.  
- Ensures team cohesion and unified policy.  
- Allows blame-sharing or credit-sharing.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Collective can shield individuals from blame.  
- Individual can lead to scapegoating.  
- In Nigeria, weak enforcement.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- UK: Collective resignation after major failures.  
- Nigeria: Ministers resign for scandals (individual); cabinet defends policies collectively.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. Collective responsibility means that  
   A. only the president is accountable  
   B. the entire cabinet accepts responsibility for government decisions  
   C. ministers act independently without coordination  
   D. no one is held accountable  
   **Answer: B**

2. In a parliamentary system, collective responsibility often leads to  
   A. automatic dissolution of parliament  
   B. resignation of the whole cabinet if defeated  
   C. individual ministers ignoring cabinet decisions  
   D. permanent tenure for ministers  
   **Answer: B**

3. In Nigeria, individual responsibility is seen when a minister  
   A. defends all government policies publicly  
   B. resigns due to personal misconduct  
   C. refuses to appear before the legislature  
   D. controls the judiciary  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Constitutionalism

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Constitutionalism is the principle that government must be conducted strictly according to the provisions of the constitution, limiting power and protecting rights. It means “limited government under law,” not arbitrary rule.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
From Magna Carta to modern bills of rights. Emphasized by Dicey (rule of law) and post-colonial states.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Government bound by constitution.  
- Supremacy of constitution.  
- Protection of rights.  
- Regular elections and peaceful transfer.

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Prevents authoritarianism.  
- Guarantees rights.  
- Promotes stability.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Can be formalistic (constitution ignored).  
- Slows urgent action.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- Strong in US. In Nigeria: 1999 Constitution embodies constitutionalism, but military interruptions and executive overreach challenge it.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. Constitutionalism primarily means  
   A. unlimited power of the government  
   B. government must operate within constitutional limits  
   C. abolition of the constitution  
   D. rule by military decree  
   **Answer: B**

2. A key feature of constitutionalism is  
   A. supremacy of personal decrees  
   B. protection of fundamental rights  
   C. suspension of elections  
   D. absolute executive power  
   **Answer: B**

3. In Nigeria, constitutionalism is challenged by  
   A. strict adherence to the 1999 Constitution  
   B. occasional executive disregard for court orders  
   C. complete absence of a constitution  
   D. unlimited judicial power  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Rule of Law

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Rule of law means no one is above the law; everyone (government and citizens) is subject to and equal before transparent, predictable laws. A.V. Dicey’s three elements: supremacy of law, equality before law, rights from court decisions.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
From Magna Carta to Dicey (1885). Core of modern democracies and UN standards.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Supremacy of law over arbitrary power.  
- Equality: No immunity except as provided.  
- Predictability: Laws clear and applied consistently.  
- Independent judiciary.  
- Fair hearing and due process.

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Protects rights and freedoms.  
- Prevents arbitrary arrest/power abuse.  
- Builds investor confidence.  
- Ensures justice.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Slow judicial process.  
- Inequality in access to justice (poor vs. rich).  
- Political interference.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- Strong in UK/US. In Nigeria: Section 36 (fair hearing), but challenges include delayed justice, executive non-compliance, and selective enforcement.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. The rule of law requires that  
   A. the president is above the law  
   B. everyone is equal before and subject to the law  
   C. laws apply only to citizens, not officials  
   D. arbitrary arrests are allowed  
   **Answer: B**

2. One element of the rule of law according to Dicey is  
   A. equality before the law  
   B. supremacy of the executive  
   C. suspension of rights during emergencies  
   D. unlimited judicial power  
   **Answer: A**

3. In Nigeria, the rule of law is undermined by  
   A. complete judicial independence  
   B. delayed justice and executive disregard for court orders  
   C. absence of any constitution  
   D. automatic enforcement of all laws  
   **Answer: B**

### Subtopic: Representative Government

**Exhaustive Definition and Explanation**  
Representative government (representative democracy) is a system where citizens elect representatives to make laws and govern on their behalf, rather than direct participation. It balances participation with efficiency in large societies.

**Historical Origins and Context**  
From British Parliament evolution to modern universal suffrage. Adopted in post-colonial states.

**Key Principles/Features**  
- Periodic free/fair elections.  
- Elected representatives (MPs, councillors).  
- Accountability through re-election or recall.  
- Pluralism (multiple parties/views).  
- Protection of minority rights.

**Merits (Advantages)**  
- Practical in large populations.  
- Allows expertise in governance.  
- Promotes stability.  
- Encourages participation via voting.

**Demerits (Disadvantages)/Challenges**  
- Representatives may ignore constituents.  
- Money/political godfathers influence.  
- Low voter turnout.  
- Risk of elite capture.

**Real-World Examples and Nigerian Relevance**  
- Most democracies (Nigeria, US, India). In Nigeria: National/State Assemblies elected every 4 years; challenges include vote-buying, low turnout, and poor representation.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. Representative government means citizens  
   A. directly make all laws  
   B. elect others to govern on their behalf  
   C. never participate in elections  
   D. rule through a monarch  
   **Answer: B**

2. A key feature of representative government is  
   A. lifetime tenure for representatives  
   B. periodic free and fair elections  
   C. abolition of political parties  
   D. direct voting on every issue  
   **Answer: B**

3. In Nigeria, representative government is practised through  
   A. military appointments  
   B. election of National and State Assemblies  
   C. hereditary succession  
   D. complete direct democracy  
   **Answer: B**
