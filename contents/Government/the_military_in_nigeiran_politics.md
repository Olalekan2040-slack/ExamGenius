**Topic 19: The Military in Nigerian Politics**  
(Part II – Political Development in Nigeria)

This topic covers one of the most defining features of Nigeria’s post-independence history: repeated military interventions that shaped governance, institutions, economy, and democracy for over 30 years (1966–1999). It is extremely important in JAMB — questions frequently require you to:

- Identify reasons/factors for military takeovers  
- Describe the structure of military regimes  
- Evaluate the impact (political, economic, social) of military rule  
- Explain processes of disengagement / handover to civilians  
- Assess achievements vs. failures

**JAMB Objectives for this Topic**  
Candidates should be able to:  
i. Evaluate the reasons given for military intervention.  
ii. Assess the achievements of military rule.  
iii. Determine the conditions that necessitated withdrawal from governance.

The subtopics are:  
a. Factors that led to military intervention  
b. Structure of military regimes  
c. Impact of military rule – political, economic, etc.  
d. Processes of military disengagement

### Subtopic a: Factors that Led to Military Intervention

**Exhaustive Explanation**

Nigeria experienced six successful military coups (and several failed attempts) between 1966 and 1993. The military cited similar justifications each time, though underlying causes were structural and systemic.

**Main Factors / Reasons Given by Coup Leaders**

1. **Corruption and Mismanagement**  
   Widespread embezzlement, nepotism, and lavish lifestyles by politicians (e.g., First Republic scandals, Second Republic “cement armada” fraud).

2. **Political Instability and Violence**  
   Rigged elections, political assassinations, ethnic/regional riots (e.g., 1964–65 election crisis, Western Region crisis 1965).

3. **Ethnic and Regional Conflicts**  
   North-South tensions, fear of domination (e.g., 1966 coup seen as Igbo-led, leading to counter-coup).

4. **Economic Mismanagement**  
   Inflation, unemployment, balance-of-payments crisis (especially pre-1983 coup).

5. **Electoral Malpractices**  
   Vote-rigging, intimidation (e.g., 1983 elections widely seen as massively rigged).

6. **Weak Institutions and Rule of Law**  
   Judiciary compromised, police partisan, no checks on executive.

7. **Military Professionalism and Ambition**  
   Officers trained to obey but saw politicians as incompetent; personal ambition (e.g., Buhari, Babangida, Abacha).

**Major Coups and Stated Reasons**

- **January 15, 1966** (Ironsi): Corruption, tribalism, election rigging.  
- **July 29, 1966** (Gowon): Revenge for perceived Igbo domination of first coup.  
- **July 29, 1975** (Murtala): Corruption, inefficiency under Gowon.  
- **December 31, 1983** (Buhari): Rigged 1983 elections, corruption, economic decay.  
- **August 27, 1985** (Babangida): Inhumanity of Buhari regime.  
- **November 17, 1993** (Abacha): Annulment crisis and instability after June 12, 1993.

**Underlying Structural Causes**  
- Ethnic/regional divisions from colonial amalgamation.  
- Weak political culture and institutions at independence.  
- Over-reliance on oil (resource curse).  
- Military politicization during civil war (1967–1970).

### Subtopic b: Structure of Military Regimes

**Exhaustive Explanation**

Military regimes in Nigeria were highly centralized, hierarchical, and personalized around the head of state. Structures evolved but shared core features.

**Common Structural Features**

1. **Supreme Military Council (SMC) / Armed Forces Ruling Council (AFRC)**  
   Highest decision-making body (later versions).  
   Chaired by Head of State; included service chiefs, senior officers.

2. **Head of State / Commander-in-Chief**  
   Absolute power; combined executive, legislative, and (partly) judicial roles.

3. **Federal Military Government (FMG)**  
   Replaced federal civilian structure; governors were military officers (Military Governors / Administrators).

4. **State Military Administrators**  
   Appointed by Head of State; ruled states with absolute powers.

5. **Ministries / Parastatals**  
   Continued under civil servants but directed by military governors/ministers.

6. **Special Bodies**  
   - National Council of States (advisory).  
   - Provisional Ruling Council (Abacha era).  
   - Transition bodies (e.g., Babangida’s Political Bureau).

**Evolution Across Regimes**  
- Ironsi (1966): Unified command, unitary decree.  
- Gowon (1966–75): SMC, 12 states created.  
- Murtala/Obasanjo (1975–79): SMC, anti-corruption, state creation (19 states).  
- Buhari (1983–85): SMC → AFRC, War Against Indiscipline.  
- Babangida (1985–93): AFRC, structural adjustment, endless transition.  
- Abacha (1993–98): Provisional Ruling Council, extreme centralization.

### Subtopic c: Impact of Military Rule

**Political Impact**

**Positive / Achievements**  
- Creation of states (from 3 regions → 12 → 19 → 21 → 30 → 36) → reduced regional domination.  
- Unification Decree (1966) → unitary system temporarily.  
- Local government reforms (1976) → grassroots democracy foundation.  
- Constitutional development (1979 Constitution – presidential system).

**Negative**  
- Suspension of constitutions and democracy.  
- Suppression of press, judiciary, opposition.  
- Human rights abuses (e.g., Abacha era executions).  
- Militarization of politics (coup culture).  
- Weakened federalism (centralized power).

**Economic Impact**

**Positive**  
- Rapid infrastructure (roads, airports under Gowon/Murtala).  
- Indigenization Decree (1972) → Nigerian control of economy.  
- Structural Adjustment Programme (1986) → liberalization (Babangida).

**Negative**  
- Corruption institutionalized (oil boom squandered).  
- Debt crisis (external loans).  
- Neglect of agriculture (oil dependency).  
- Privatization mismanagement.

**Social Impact**  
- War Against Indiscipline (WAI) → temporary order.  
- Increased ethnic tensions (1966 coups/pogroms).  
- Brain drain (professionals fled).  
- Decline in education/health standards.

### Subtopic d: Processes of Military Disengagement

**Exhaustive Explanation**

All military regimes (except Abacha’s) eventually announced transition programmes to civilian rule, though many were prolonged or aborted.

**Common Steps in Disengagement**

1. **Announcement of Transition Programme**  
   Setting timeline (usually 2–5 years).

2. **Constitution Review / Drafting**  
   Constituent Assembly or committee (e.g., 1978 CDC for 1979 Constitution).

3. **Political Transition Bodies**  
   Political Bureau (Babangida), Constitutional Conference (Abacha).

4. **Party Formation and Registration**  
   Controlled (e.g., Babangida’s two-party system: SDP/NRC).

5. **Local / State / National Elections**  
   Step-by-step (local first).

6. **Handover Ceremony**  
   Power transfer to elected president.

**Successful Disengagements**  
- 1979: Murtala/Obasanjo → Shehu Shagari (Second Republic).  
- 1999: Abubakar → Olusegun Obasanjo (Fourth Republic).

**Failed / Aborted**  
- Babangida: Annulled June 12, 1993 (MKO Abiola presumed winner).  
- Abacha: Died before handover (1998).

**Conditions that Necessitated Withdrawal**  
- Internal pressure (economic failure, protests).  
- External pressure (sanctions, debt relief conditions).  
- Military fatigue / factionalism.  
- Death of leader (Abacha).  
- Elite consensus for democracy.

**JAMB-Style Objective Questions (4 options each)**

1. One major reason given for military intervention in Nigeria in 1966 was  
   A. too much democracy  
   B. corruption, election rigging, and political violence  
   C. excessive economic growth  
   D. complete absence of ethnic conflict  
   **Answer: B**

2. The creation of 12 states in 1967 under Gowon was a significant  
   A. economic achievement  
   B. political impact of military rule to reduce regional dominance  
   C. social welfare programme  
   D. judicial reform  
   **Answer: B**

3. The military regime that annulled the June 12, 1993 election was  
   A. Gowon  
   B. Buhari  
   C. Babangida  
   D. Abacha  
   **Answer: C**

4. A major condition that forced military disengagement in 1999 was  
   A. sudden economic boom  
   B. death of Abacha and domestic/international pressure  
   C. complete success of transition programme  
   D. absence of any opposition  
   **Answer: B**

**Summary & Quick Revision Tip**  
- Reasons: Corruption, instability, rigging, ethnic tension.  
- Structure: Highly centralized (SMC/AFRC → Head of State → Military Governors).  
- Impact: State creation, infrastructure (positive); authoritarianism, corruption, debt (negative).  
- Disengagement: Transition programmes, elections, handover (successful 1979 & 1999).

Mnemonic for coups: **1966 Jan – Ironsi (corruption)** → **1966 Jul – Gowon (revenge)** → **1975 – Murtala (Gowon inefficiency)** → **1983 – Buhari (1983 rigging)** → **1985 – Babangida (Buhari inhumanity)** → **1993 – Abacha (annulment crisis)**.
