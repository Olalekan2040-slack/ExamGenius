**Topic 4: Constitutional Development in Nigeria**

This topic traces the evolution of Nigeria's constitutional framework from the colonial era to independence, showing how each document built on the previous one in response to political pressures, nationalist demands, and British colonial strategies. Constitutional development was not isolated but influenced by preceding events such as World War I (demands for representation), the rise of nationalism (1920s elite agitation), World War II (global self-determination ideas), and post-war conferences (e.g., Ibadan 1948). These led to gradual reforms from limited representation to full federalism and sovereignty. The process was "top-down" (British-imposed initially) but increasingly consultative with Nigerian input.

### a. Hugh Clifford Constitution (1922)

**Detailed Background and Preceding Events**  
The Clifford Constitution was introduced in 1922 by Governor Sir Hugh Clifford, marking Nigeria's first significant step toward representative governance. Preceding events included the 1914 Amalgamation of Northern and Southern Protectorates by Lord Lugard, which created a unified Nigeria but lacked African input, leading to growing resentment. World War I (1914–1918) was pivotal: Nigerians served in the British army, returning with ideas of self-determination from Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points (1918). The 1919 Versailles Treaty and League of Nations mandates further inspired educated elites (e.g., Lagos lawyers) to demand representation. The National Congress of British West Africa (NCBWA) in 1920 petitioned for reforms, pressuring Britain. Clifford's constitution was a limited response to these demands, aiming to pacify elites without ceding real power.

**Key Features**  
- Introduced elective principle for the first time (Africans could elect representatives).  
- Legislative Council expanded: 46 members (26 officials, 20 unofficials including 4 elected from Lagos (3) and Calabar (1) – based on £100 income/property qualification).  
- Council legislated only for the Colony (Lagos and Calabar); Northern/Southern Protectorates excluded and ruled by proclamation.  
- Executive Council remained all-British and advisory to the Governor.  
- Governor retained veto power over all legislation.

**Merits**  
- Pioneered elections and African representation, empowering educated elites.  
- Stimulated political awareness and formation of early parties (e.g., NNDP in 1923).  
- Laid groundwork for future reforms by introducing legislative debate.

**Demerits**  
- Extremely limited franchise (only 4 elected seats, high qualifications excluded 99% of population).  
- Excluded the vast Protectorates (over 90% of Nigeria), perpetuating regional divides.  
- No real power for Africans (Governor's veto made Council ineffective).  
- Imposed without consultation, seen as tokenism.

### b. Arthur Richards Constitution (1946)

**Detailed Background and Preceding Events**  
The Richards Constitution was introduced in 1946 by Governor Sir Arthur Richards (later Lord Milverton) as part of Britain's post-World War II colonial reforms. Preceding events were critical: WWII (1939–1945) saw over 100,000 Nigerians fight for Britain, returning with heightened nationalist expectations inspired by the Atlantic Charter (1941 – Churchill/Roosevelt promise of self-determination). The 1945 General Strike in Nigeria (led by Michael Imoudu) demanded better wages and political reforms. The NCNC's 1945 tour and protests against colonial policies pressured Britain. Globally, India's independence movement and UN formation (1945) accelerated decolonization. Richards aimed to "Nigerianize" administration without full self-rule, introducing regionalism to manage diversity.

**Key Features**  
- Introduced regionalism: Three regions (North, East, West) with Regional Houses of Assembly (advisory roles).  
- Central Legislative Council: 45 members (unofficial majority – 28 Africans, but mostly nominated/chiefs).  
- Regional representatives in central Council.  
- Executive Council still British-dominated.  
- Governor retained reserve powers and veto.

**Merits**  
- Recognised Nigeria's regional diversity, laying foundation for federalism.  
- Increased African participation (unofficial majority in Council).  
- Promoted regional development and autonomy.  
- Responded to nationalist demands for more inclusion.

**Demerits**  
- No responsible government (Governor still supreme, no ministerial system).  
- Regional assemblies were advisory only, with no real power.  
- Unequal representation (North given parity despite population debates, favouring North).  
- Imposed without Nigerian consultation (Richards drafted it alone), leading to protests and rejection by nationalists as "divide and rule."

### c. John Macpherson Constitution (1951)

**Detailed Background and Preceding Events**  
The Macpherson Constitution was enacted in 1951 by Governor Sir John Macpherson, marking a shift toward more consultative reforms. Preceding events included the 1946 Richards Constitution's rejection by nationalists (e.g., NCNC "Freedom Charter" tour), leading to riots and demands for self-rule. The 1947–1948 Ibadan General Conference allowed Nigerian input for the first time. WWII's aftermath, India's independence (1947), and Gold Coast (Ghana) reforms (1946) pressured Britain. The 1949 Enugu Colliery Shooting (killing 21 miners) intensified labour/nationalist unrest. Macpherson's constitution was a compromise to introduce ministerial responsibility and regional self-government.

**Key Features**  
- Bicameral legislatures in regions: House of Assembly (elected) + House of Chiefs (North/West).  
- Central House of Representatives (136 members, regional representation).  
- Ministerial system: Nigerians as ministers responsible to legislatures (10 at centre, 12 in regions).  
- Regional Executives with Premiers (though Governor still head).  
- Governor retained reserve powers for emergencies.

**Merits**  
- Introduced responsible government with Nigerian ministers.  
- Enhanced regional autonomy (regions gained self-government).  
- Increased elected representation and democratic participation.  
- Prepared Nigerians for leadership roles.

**Demerits**  
- Central government still not fully responsible (Governor's reserves).  
- Regional imbalances and ethnic tensions worsened (e.g., motion for self-government in 1953 led to crisis).  
- Weak federal structure (regions too powerful).  
- High illiteracy limited effective participation.

### d. Oliver Lyttleton Constitution (1954)

**Detailed Background and Preceding Events**  
The Lyttleton Constitution was introduced in 1954 by Colonial Secretary Oliver Lyttleton (Lord Chandos) after the 1953 constitutional crisis. Preceding events: The 1953 self-government motion by Anthony Enahoro (AG) in House of Representatives caused riots when Northern members opposed it (Kano riots killed 36). This led to the 1953 London Constitutional Conference, where leaders (Azikiwe, Awolowo, Bello) agreed on federalism. The Macpherson's weaknesses (e.g., no true federalism) were exposed. Ghana's self-government (1951) and global decolonization pressures accelerated reforms. Lyttleton's constitution was a federal compromise to prevent breakup.

**Key Features**  
- Full federalism: Three regions (North, East, West) + Lagos as federal territory.  
- Legislative lists: Exclusive (federal), concurrent (shared), residual (regions).  
- Regional Premiers with self-government (East/West 1957, North 1959).  
- Federal Prime Minister (Tafawa Balewa from 1957).  
- Bicameral federal legislature: House of Representatives (elected) + Senate (regional nominees).  
- Governor-General (ceremonial head representing Queen).

**Merits**  
- Established true federalism with power-sharing.  
- Granted regional self-government, preparing for independence.  
- Reduced central tensions by decentralising power.  
- Introduced Prime Ministerial system at federal level.

**Demerits**  
- Still colonial (Queen/Governor-General as head).  
- Regional imbalances (North's size gave it more House seats).  
- Ethnic/regional parties (NPC, NCNC, AG) deepened divisions.  
- No bill of rights initially.

### e. Independence Constitution (1960)

**Detailed Background and Preceding Events**  
The 1960 Independence Constitution was the culmination of nationalist struggles and negotiations. Preceding events: The 1957–1958 London Constitutional Conferences resolved final issues (e.g., regional self-government, minority rights). The 1959 federal elections saw NPC-NCNC coalition win. Global context: Wave of African independence (Ghana 1957, Guinea 1958). Nationalist parties' agitation (e.g., Azikiwe's NCNC demands) and British willingness to decolonise post-Suez Crisis (1956) led to October 1, 1960 independence. The constitution was essentially the Lyttleton framework with sovereignty added.

**Key Features**  
- Full sovereignty (Nigeria independent, Queen as ceremonial head via Governor-General).  
- Parliamentary system: Prime Minister (head of government) from majority party/coalition.  
- Federal structure retained: Three regions + federal capital Lagos.  
- Bicameral legislature: House of Representatives (312 elected) + Senate (44 nominated by regions).  
- Fundamental human rights included (first time).  
- Judicial independence with Privy Council appeals.

**Merits**  
- Achieved full political independence.  
- Protected rights and federal balance.  
- Allowed regional development.  
- Parliamentary system promoted debate.

**Demerits**  
- Regional dominance (North controlled federal government due to size).  
- Ethnic-based parties led to instability (e.g., 1962–1965 crises).  
- Weak centre (regions too autonomous).  
- Monarchical elements (Queen as head) until 1963 Republic.

**Summary & Quick Revision Tip**  
- Progression: Limited representation → Regionalism → Ministerial system → Federalism → Independence.  
- Each constitution was more progressive than the previous.  
- Key driver: Nationalist agitation + British gradual withdrawal.
