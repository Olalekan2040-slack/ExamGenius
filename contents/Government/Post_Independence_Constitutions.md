### 5. Post-Independence Constitutions

This section covers Nigeria's constitutions after independence in 1960, reflecting the country's struggle to balance federalism, democracy, and stability amid ethnic tensions, regional rivalries, military interventions, and demands for restructuring. Each constitution built on the previous one, often in response to crises like the 1966 coups, the civil war (1967–1970), and failed transitions. The 1963 was republican; 1979 shifted to presidential; 1989 was aborted; 1999 is current but criticised as military-imposed. These documents show Nigeria's evolution from parliamentary to presidential system and from 3 regions to 36 states.

#### Subtopics
- **1963 Republican Constitution**: Characteristics (parliamentary, ceremonial president, regional focus); shortcomings (regional imbalances leading to instability).  
- **1979 Presidential Constitution**: Characteristics (executive president, federal character, bicameral assembly); shortcomings (corruption and electoral fraud).  
- **1989 Constitution (Aborted Third Republic)**: Characteristics (two-party system, state increase); shortcomings (military manipulation and annulment).  
- **1999 Constitution (Fourth Republic – Current)**: Characteristics (presidential, fundamental rights, local government recognition); shortcomings (over-centralisation, lack of true federalism).

#### Learning Objectives
Candidates should follow this orderly sequence:  
1. Describe the characteristics of each post-independence constitution, noting key changes from previous ones.  
2. Assess the workings of the constitutions (e.g., how the 1979 presidential system aimed to prevent parliamentary gridlock).  
3. Evaluate shortcomings and why they led to failure or ongoing issues (e.g., 1999's military origins fuel restructuring calls).  
4. Compare the constitutions chronologically, analysing how they addressed Nigeria's challenges like ethnicity, federalism, and democracy.
