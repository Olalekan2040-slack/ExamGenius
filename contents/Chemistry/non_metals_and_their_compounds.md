TOPIC: Non-metals and Their CompoundsIntroductionNon-metals are elements that are not metals – they are usually poor conductors of heat and electricity, brittle, and many are gases or solids at room temperature. The important non-metals for JAMB are hydrogen, halogens (group 17: F, Cl, Br, I), oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon.  We study their properties (physical and chemical), how their compounds are prepared in the lab or industry, and their uses in everyday life.  For a complete beginner: Think of non-metals as the "opposite" of metals. Metals are shiny and conduct electricity; non-metals are dull, do not conduct, and many form important gases like O₂ (for breathing) and N₂ (in air).This topic comes in JAMB with 4–6 questions every year. They ask about:Physical states and colours of halogens  
Laboratory preparation of gases (H₂, O₂, NH₃, CO₂)  
Allotropes of carbon (diamond, graphite)  
Tests for gases (like limewater for CO₂)

Hydrogen is the lightest element (H₂ gas). It is colourless, odourless, and very flammable. It burns with a blue flame and produces water.Key Properties:Physical: Colourless, odourless gas, lightest gas  
Chemical: Highly reactive with oxygen (burns/explodes), reduces metal oxides

Laboratory Preparation:
Zinc + dilute HCl or H₂SO₄ → H₂ gas + salt

Thinking hydrogen is heavier than air (no – collected by downward displacement)

SUBTOPIC: Halogens (Group 17)Properties, Preparation, and UsesExplanation:
Halogens are reactive non-metals: fluorine (F₂), chlorine (Cl₂), bromine (Br₂), iodine (I₂). They gain electrons easily to form halide ions (F⁻, Cl⁻, etc.).Key Properties:Reactivity decreases down the group (F most reactive, I least)  
Physical states: F₂ and Cl₂ gas, Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid  
Colours: F pale yellow, Cl greenish-yellow, Br red-brown, I purple vapour

Preparation (for chlorine – common):
Oxidation of HCl with MnO₂ or electrolysis of brineUses:Chlorine: Water treatment (kills germs), bleaching  
Iodine: Antiseptic (tincture of iodine)  
Fluorine: In toothpaste (prevents tooth decay)

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "Which halogen is a liquid at room temperature? A) Chlorine B) Bromine C) Iodine D) Fluorine" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Saying iodine is a gas (it sublimes to purple vapour but solid at room temp)

SUBTOPIC: OxygenProperties, Preparation, and UsesExplanation:
Oxygen (O₂) is a colourless, odourless gas, supports combustion (makes things burn better).Key Properties:Slightly soluble in water  
Relights glowing splint (test for oxygen)

Laboratory Preparation:
Heat potassium chlorate (KClO₃) with manganese dioxide (MnO₂) catalyst
2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂↑Here are lab diagrams for preparation of oxygen:This picture shows the classic setup: KClO₃ + MnO₂ in test tube, heated, gas collected over water.This one labels the apparatus clearly.Uses:Breathing (in hospitals)  
Welding (oxy-acetylene flame)  
Steel making

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "The gas that relights a glowing splint is: A) Hydrogen B) Oxygen C) Carbon dioxide D) Nitrogen" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking oxygen is collected by upward displacement (no – downward because slightly soluble)

SUBTOPIC: Nitrogen and Its CompoundsProperties, Preparation, and UsesExplanation:
Nitrogen (N₂) is the major gas in air (78%). Very stable, does not burn.Important Compound: Ammonia (NH₃)  Colourless gas with pungent smell  
Prepared by Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (high pressure, 450°C, iron catalyst)

Here is the Haber process diagram (very important!):This shows the industrial setup for making ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen.Uses of NH₃:Making fertilisers (ammonium nitrate, urea)  
Refrigerant  
Cleaning agent

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "The catalyst used in Haber process is: A) Platinum B) Iron C) Manganese dioxide D) Vanadium" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking ammonia is made from air directly (needs high pressure and catalyst)
