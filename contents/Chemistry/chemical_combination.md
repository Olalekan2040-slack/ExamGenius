TOPIC: Chemical CombinationIntroductionChemical combination explores the fundamental laws governing how elements and compounds interact and combine in fixed ratios, forming the basis for understanding chemical reactions and stoichiometry. It also introduces the mole concept, which quantifies substances at the atomic level for precise calculations in chemistry. JAMB typically includes 3-4 questions on this topic, emphasizing calculations involving laws of combination and mole-based stoichiometry to test foundational quantitative skills.SUBTOPIC: Laws of Chemical CombinationObjective 1: Perform calculations based on laws of chemical combinationExplanation: The laws of chemical combination are empirical rules derived from experiments that describe how elements combine to form compounds in predictable ways. These laws ensure that chemical reactions follow consistent patterns, such as fixed mass ratios or conservation of mass, which are essential for balancing equations and predicting outcomes in reactions. Understanding them helps in verifying compound compositions and solving problems related to proportional masses in chemistry.Key Points:Law of definite proportions states that a compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of the source.
Law of multiple proportions applies when two elements form more than one compound, with the ratios of masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other being simple whole numbers.
Law of conservation of matter (or mass) asserts that in a chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products, as matter is neither created nor destroyed.
Law of reciprocal proportions indicates that when two elements combine separately with a third element, the ratio of their masses is the same or a multiple of the ratio when they combine with each other.

Common Mistakes:Confusing definite proportions with multiple proportions by not recognizing variable compounds between the same elements.
Ignoring units in mass ratio calculations, leading to incorrect application of conservation of mass.
Misapplying reciprocal proportions by not relating ratios correctly between multiple elements.

SUBTOPIC: Mole Concept and Avogadro's NumberObjective 1: Use mole concept in stoichiometric calculationsExplanation: The mole concept represents a standard amount of substance (6.022 × 10²³ particles) that allows chemists to count atoms or molecules by weighing macroscopic quantities. Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance, enabling conversions between mass, moles, and particles. This is crucial for stoichiometry, where moles help balance equations and predict reactant/product quantities in reactions.Key Points:A mole is defined as the amount of substance containing Avogadro's number of particles, equivalent to the atomic/molecular mass in grams.
Molar mass (M) is calculated from the periodic table (e.g., H₂O = 18 g/mol).
Stoichiometric calculations involve mole ratios from balanced equations, such as finding limiting reactants or yields.

Card 3: Conversion Chain - Grams → Moles → Particles (using Avogadro's number).

Example Question Pattern: Question 1: "How many moles are in 36g of water (H₂O, molar mass 18g/mol)? A) 1 B) 2 C) 18 D) 36" (Answer: B)
Question 2: "In the reaction N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, how many moles of NH₃ are produced from 2 moles of N₂? A) 1 B) 2 C) 3 D) 4" (Answer: D)
Question 3: "Calculate the mass of 0.5 moles of CO₂ (molar mass 44g/mol). A) 22g B) 44g C) 88g D) 11g" (Answer: A)

Common Mistakes:Forgetting to use molar mass correctly, such as using atomic mass instead of molecular mass.
Misinterpreting mole ratios in unbalanced equations, leading to wrong stoichiometric proportions.

Objective 2: Relate Avogadro's number to particle quantitiesExplanation: Avogadro's number (6.022 × 10²³) is the constant that links the microscopic world of atoms to measurable macroscopic quantities, defining one mole. It allows calculation of the number of atoms, ions, or molecules in a given mass of substance. This relation is key for understanding scale in chemistry, from gas volumes to solution concentrations.Key Points:Avogadro's number represents the number of particles in one mole of any substance.
Calculations: Number of particles = moles × Avogadro's number.
Significance: It standardizes counting in chemistry, e.g., 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 dm³ at STP.

Question 3: "What is the significance of Avogadro's number in relating mass to particles? A) Defines temperature B) Links moles to volume C) Counts particles per mole D) Measures density" (Answer: C)

Common Mistakes:Using Avogadro's number without multiplying by moles, resulting in overestimating particle counts.
Confusing Avogadro's number with molar volume, especially in gas-related problems.
