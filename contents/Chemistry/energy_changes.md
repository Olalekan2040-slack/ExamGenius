TOPIC: Energy ChangesIntroductionThis topic explains how energy is involved in every chemical reaction. Some reactions give out heat (they feel hot), while others take in heat (they feel cold). We use special words and signs to describe these energy changes.  For a complete beginner: Imagine reactions as people giving or taking money (energy). Some reactions are generous (give out energy = exothermic), some are selfish (take energy = endothermic).  This is a very important JAMB topic – usually 3–5 questions every year. They ask you to:  Identify if a reaction is exothermic or endothermic  
Understand enthalpy change (∆H)  
Use Hess's law to calculate energy  
Explain when a reaction will happen on its own (spontaneity) using ∆H, ∆S and ∆G

Every reaction has an energy change. We call this change enthalpy change and write it as ∆H (delta H – delta means "change").Two main types:Exothermic reactions  Heat is given out (released) to the surroundings  
The reaction container feels hot  
∆H is negative (–)  
Example: Burning of fuel (wood, petrol), respiration in our body, rusting of iron

Endothermic reactions  Heat is taken in (absorbed) from the surroundings  
The reaction container feels cold  
∆H is positive (+)  
Example: Photosynthesis, dissolving some salts like NH₄NO₃, cooking an egg (protein denaturation)

Easy memory trick:
Exo = Exit → heat exits → negative ∆H
Endo = Enter → heat enters → positive ∆HKey Points:Exothermic: ∆H < 0 (negative), heat released  
Endothermic: ∆H > 0 (positive), heat absorbed  
Most combustion, neutralisation, and respiration reactions are exothermic  
Most decomposition and photosynthesis are endothermic

Confusing heat absorbed by reaction with heat absorbed by surroundings

SUBTOPIC: Enthalpy Changes (∆H) & Hess's LawObjective 2: Use Hess's law to calculate enthalpy changesExplanation (simple for beginners):
Hess's law says:
"The total heat change (enthalpy change) for a reaction is the same whether the reaction takes place in one step or in many steps."In simple words:
It doesn't matter if you go from A → B directly or through many steps (A → C → D → B), the total energy change is always the same.This is very useful because some reactions are hard to measure directly, so we measure easier steps and add them up.How to use Hess's law (easy steps):Draw the energy cycle (arrow diagram)  
Write the equation you want  
Write alternative steps that add up to the same overall reaction  
Add up the ∆H values (watch the direction – reverse = change sign)

SUBTOPIC: Entropy and Gibbs Free EnergyObjective 3: Relate ∆H, ∆S, ∆G to spontaneityExplanation (made very simple for beginners):
Not all reactions with negative ∆H happen by themselves. Some need help (heating, etc.).We use Gibbs free energy (∆G) to decide if a reaction will happen on its own (spontaneous).The formula every JAMB student must know:
∆G = ∆H – T∆S
(Where T is temperature in Kelvin)Three rules to remember:If ∆G is negative (–) → reaction is spontaneous (happens on its own)  
If ∆G is positive (+) → reaction is not spontaneous (needs help)  
If ∆G = 0 → reaction is at equilibrium (no change)

What is entropy (∆S)?
Entropy is a measure of disorder or randomness.  More disorder (gas from solid, more molecules) → ∆S positive (+)  
Less disorder (solid from gas) → ∆S negative (–)

Simple rule for spontaneity:If ∆H negative (exothermic) + ∆S positive (more disorder) → always spontaneous  
If ∆H positive + ∆S negative → never spontaneous  
Mixed cases → depends on temperature
