TOPIC: ElectrolysisIntroductionElectrolysis is a very important process in chemistry where we use electricity to break down a compound (usually a solution or molten substance) into its elements or simpler parts.
It is like using electric power to force a chemical reaction that would not happen on its own.  For beginners: Think of it as "electricity splitting things apart" – just like how we use electricity to split water into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.  This topic comes up a lot in JAMB – usually 3-5 questions. They ask about Faraday's laws (how much substance is produced), the difference between electrolytic and electrochemical cells, and real-life uses like electroplating (coating things with metal) and purification of copper. Michael Faraday discovered two important laws about electrolysis. They help us calculate how much substance (like metal or gas) is produced when electricity passes through an electrolyte.Faraday's First Law (very easy):
The amount of substance produced or used up at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity (charge) passed through the electrolyte.
In simple words: More electricity = more substance produced.Faraday's Second Law (also easy):
When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses of substances produced are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights (or atomic mass divided by valency).
In simple words: Different substances need different amounts of electricity to produce 1 gram equivalent.Key formula every JAMB student must know (learn by heart):Mass (m) = (Q × M) / (n × F)  Where:  m = mass deposited (in grams)  
Q = quantity of electricity = Current (I) × Time (t)  
M = molar mass of substance  
n = number of electrons transferred (valency)  
F = Faraday constant = 96,500 C/mol (approximately 96,500 coulombs per mole)

Key Points:1 Faraday (96,500 C) deposits 1 mole of monovalent ion (like Ag⁺), or ½ mole of divalent (like Cu²⁺), etc.  
Always use SI units: Current in amperes, time in seconds.

Using wrong value of n (number of electrons) – e.g., for Cu²⁺ n=2, for Ag⁺ n=1.  
Mixing up Faraday's first and second laws.

SUBTOPIC: Electrolytic and Electrochemical CellsObjective 2: Explain the difference between electrolytic and electrochemical cellsExplanation (very simple):
There are two types of cells that use electricity and chemistry together:Electrolytic cell – we supply electricity (from battery) to force a non-spontaneous reaction (like splitting water or electroplating). Electricity → chemical reaction.  
Electrochemical cell (also called galvanic or voltaic cell) – the chemical reaction happens spontaneously and produces electricity (like in batteries or dry cells). Chemical reaction → electricity.

SUBTOPIC: Applications (Electroplating, Purification)Objective 3: Explain electrolysis applicationsExplanation (real-life uses – very important for JAMB):
Electrolysis is used in industries for two main things beginners should know well:Electroplating – coating a cheap metal object with a thin layer of expensive metal (like silver, gold, chrome) to make it look good and prevent rust.
Example: Iron spoon coated with silver to make silver spoon.
Purification of metals – especially copper. We use impure copper as anode, pure copper as cathode, and CuSO₄ solution. Impurities fall as sludge, pure copper deposits on cathode.

Key Points:In electroplating: Object to be plated = cathode (-), metal to coat with = anode (+).  
In copper purification: Anode dissolves (impure Cu → Cu²⁺), cathode grows pure Cu.

Here are real-life pictures of applications:This diagram shows electroplating – object at cathode gets coated with shiny metal.This illustration shows purification of copper – impure anode, pure cathode, and anode slime (impurities).Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "In electroplating of silver on iron, the iron object is connected to: A) Anode B) Cathode C) Both D) None" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "During purification of copper by electrolysis, impurities collect as: A) Cathode deposit B) Anode slime/sludge C) Solution D) Gas" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking the object to be plated is anode (no – it's cathode).  
Forgetting that in purification, anode is impure metal.
