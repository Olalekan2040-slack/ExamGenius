TOPIC: Acids, Bases, and SaltsIntroductionThis topic explains what acids and bases are, how they behave, and how they react to form salts. Acids taste sour (like lemon), bases feel soapy and taste bitter, while salts are neutral products from their reaction. It is one of the most important topics in JAMB Chemistry because it appears in almost every exam paper – usually 4-6 questions. This chart shows litmus (red in acid, blue in base), phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in base), and methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base).SUBTOPIC: Definitions and PropertiesObjective 1: Distinguish between acid-base theoriesExplanation: There are three main theories that explain what acids and bases are. Arrhenius theory (oldest) says acids give H⁺ ions in water, bases give OH⁻ ions. Bronsted-Lowry theory is wider – acid donates proton (H⁺), base accepts proton. Lewis theory is the most general – acid accepts electron pair, base donates electron pair. In JAMB, you must know which theory applies to different examples.Key Points:Arrhenius: Acids produce H⁺ in water (e.g., HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻), bases produce OH⁻ (e.g., NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻). Limited to water solutions.
Bronsted-Lowry: Acid donates H⁺, base accepts H⁺ (e.g., NH₃ accepts H⁺ to become NH₄⁺). Includes non-aqueous reactions.
Lewis: Acid accepts lone pair of electrons (e.g., BF₃ accepts from NH₃), base donates lone pair. Widest theory.
Properties of acids: Sour taste, turn blue litmus red, react with metals to give H₂, react with bases to form salt + water.
Properties of bases: Bitter taste, soapy feel, turn red litmus blue, react with acids to form salt + water.

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "According to Bronsted-Lowry theory, NH₃ acts as a base because it: A) Produces OH⁻ B) Accepts H⁺ C) Accepts electron pair D) Donates H⁺" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "BF₃ + NH₃ → BF₃NH₃ is an example of: A) Arrhenius acid B) Bronsted-Lowry base C) Lewis acid-base reaction D) Arrhenius base" (Answer: C)  
Question 3: "Which theory defines acid as a proton donor? A) Arrhenius B) Lewis C) Bronsted-Lowry D) All" (Answer: C)  
Question 4: "The property common to all acids is: A) Sour taste B) Produce H⁺ in water C) Turn litmus red D) React with metals" (Answer: C – most reliable test)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking Arrhenius theory works for all acids/bases (it only works in water).  
Confusing who donates or accepts in Bronsted-Lowry (acid donates H⁺, base accepts).  
Saying Lewis acid donates electrons (no, it accepts).

SUBTOPIC: SaltsObjective 2: Prepare and classify saltsExplanation: Salts are ionic compounds formed when acids react with bases (neutralisation). Types include normal salts (from complete neutralisation), acidic salts (still have replaceable H⁺), and basic salts (still have replaceable OH⁻). Preparation methods include: acid + base, acid + metal, acid + carbonate, acid + metal oxide.Here are laboratory diagrams showing preparation of salts by neutralisation (e.g., making copper sulphate or sodium chloride):edu.rsc.org

This shows common salts like NaCl (table salt), NaHCO₃ (baking soda), NH₄Cl (sal ammoniac).Key Points:Normal salts: Complete neutralisation, no H⁺ or OH⁻ left (e.g., NaCl, CuSO₄)  
Acidic salts: From polyprotic acids, still have H⁺ (e.g., NaHSO₄, NaHCO₃)  
Basic salts: Have extra OH⁻ or basic oxide (e.g., Mg(OH)Cl)  
Preparation methods:  Acid + base → salt + water (titration)  
Acid + metal → salt + H₂  
Acid + carbonate → salt + CO₂ + H₂O  
Acid + metal oxide → salt + H₂O

Uses: NaCl – food; Na₂CO₃ – soap; CuSO₄ – fungicide; NH₄Cl – batteries

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "The salt formed when HCl reacts completely with NaOH is: A) NaHSO₄ B) NaCl C) NaHCO₃ D) Na₂SO₄" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "Which is an acidic salt? A) NaCl B) NaHSO₄ C) K₂SO₄ D) CaCO₃" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "To prepare soluble salt like NaCl from NaOH and HCl, the method is: A) Precipitation B) Titration and crystallisation C) Direct combination D) Thermal decomposition" (Answer: B)  
Question 4: "The gas produced when acid reacts with carbonate is: A) H₂ B) O₂ C) CO₂ D) NH₃" (Answer: C)

Common Mistakes:  Calling NaHCO₃ a normal salt (it is acidic because it can still donate H⁺).  
Forgetting to evaporate and crystallise after neutralisation.  
Mixing up acidic and basic salts (acidic has extra H⁺, basic has extra base).
