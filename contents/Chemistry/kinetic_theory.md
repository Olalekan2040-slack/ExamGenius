TOPIC: Kinetic Theory of Matter and Gas LawsIntroductionThis topic explains how tiny particles (atoms or molecules) move and behave in solids, liquids, and gases, and how this movement controls the properties of gases like pressure, volume, and temperature. The kinetic theory helps us understand everyday things like why a tyre becomes harder when hot or why gases spread out quickly. JAMB usually asks 3-5 questions from this topic, focusing on explaining gas behaviour using kinetic theory and doing calculations with gas laws. These pictures show the arrangement clearly: particles in solid are tightly packed and vibrate in place, in liquid they are close but can slide over each other, and in gas they are far apart and move freely.SUBTOPIC: Kinetic TheoryObjective 1: Use kinetic theory to explain gas behaviourExplanation: The kinetic theory says that all matter is made up of very small particles (atoms or molecules) that are always moving. In gases, these particles move very fast in all directions, hit the walls of the container, and that is what causes pressure. The faster they move (higher temperature), the higher the pressure. This simple idea explains many things about gases.Key Points:All matter consists of tiny moving particles.
In gases, particles are far apart and move randomly at high speed.
Pressure is caused by particles constantly hitting the walls of the container.
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy (speed) of the particles.

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "According to kinetic theory, the pressure of a gas increases when temperature rises because: A) particles become bigger B) particles move faster C) particles become fewer D) particles stop moving" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "Why do gases exert pressure on the walls of their container? A) Gravity pulls them down B) Particles collide with the walls C) Particles attract each other D) Particles are fixed" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "Kinetic theory assumes that gas particles: A) have strong forces between them B) are very large C) do not attract each other D) are always at rest" (Answer: C)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking pressure is caused by gravity instead of particle collisions.  
Forgetting that particles in gas have no attraction between them (ideal gas assumption).  
Mixing up temperature with heat – temperature measures average kinetic energy.

This picture shows how smoke particles move randomly because fast-moving air molecules keep hitting them.Objective 2: Explain states of matter using kinetic theory (solid, liquid, gas)Explanation: The same kinetic theory explains the three states of matter. In solids, particles vibrate but stay in fixed positions. In liquids, particles can move around but stay close. In gases, particles move freely and fast in all directions. The difference is mainly in the strength of attraction between particles and their energy.Key Points:Solids: Strong attraction, particles vibrate in fixed places.  
Liquids: Weaker attraction, particles slide past each other.  
Gases: Very weak or no attraction, particles move freely and far apart.  
Diffusion and Brownian motion are faster in gases than in liquids.

Common Mistakes:  Saying solids have no movement at all (they vibrate).  
Thinking gas particles do not collide with each other.

SUBTOPIC: Gas LawsObjective 1: Solve problems involving gas lawsExplanation: Gas laws show the relationship between pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and amount of gas. They come directly from kinetic theory. Boyle's law says if temperature is constant, pressure and volume are opposite (P ∝ 1/V). Charles's law says if pressure is constant, volume increases with temperature (V ∝ T). Pressure law says if volume is constant, pressure increases with temperature.Here is Boyle's law graph (pressure increases as volume decreases at constant temperature):pressbooks.bccampus.ca

And here is Charles's law graph (volume increases as temperature increases at constant pressure):byjus.com

Key Points:Boyle's law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (constant T)  
Charles's law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (constant P, T in Kelvin)  
Pressure law: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ (constant V, T in Kelvin)  
Always change temperature to Kelvin (K = °C + 273)

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "A gas occupies 400 cm³ at 2 atm. What will be its volume at 1 atm if temperature is constant? A) 200 cm³ B) 800 cm³ C) 400 cm³ D) 100 cm³" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "A gas has volume 500 cm³ at 27°C. What will be the volume at 127°C if pressure is constant? A) 250 cm³ B) 600 cm³ C) 1000 cm³ D) 700 cm³" (Answer: B) (Note: 27°C = 300 K, 127°C = 400 K)  
Question 3: "If a gas pressure is 3 atm at 100 K, what will be the pressure at 200 K if volume is constant? A) 1.5 atm B) 6 atm C) 3 atm D) 4 atm" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Forgetting to convert °C to Kelvin before using Charles's or pressure law.  
Using wrong formula for Boyle's law (P ∝ V instead of P ∝ 1/V).

Objective 2: Perform calculations with ideal gas equation and other lawsExplanation: The ideal gas equation PV = nRT combines all gas laws into one formula. It works well for most gases at normal conditions. Dalton's law says total pressure of mixture of gases = sum of partial pressures. Graham's law says lighter gases diffuse faster than heavier ones.Here is an illustration of Graham's law showing two gases diffusing at different rates:byjus.com

Key Points:Ideal gas equation: PV = nRT (R = 0.0821 dm³ atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)  
Dalton's law: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃...  
Graham's law: Rate₁ / Rate₂ = √(M₂ / M₁) where M = molar mass  
Diffusion is faster for lighter gases (e.g., ammonia faster than HCl).

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "Calculate the volume occupied by 2 moles of oxygen at 27°C and 1 atm (R = 0.0821 dm³ atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹). A) 24.6 dm³ B) 49.2 dm³ C) 98.4 dm³ D) 12.3 dm³" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "If hydrogen (H₂, M=2) and oxygen (O₂, M=32) diffuse, what is the ratio of their diffusion rates? A) 1:4 B) 4:1 C) 1:16 D) 16:1" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "In a mixture, partial pressure of N₂ is 0.6 atm and O₂ is 0.4 atm. What is the total pressure? A) 0.2 atm B) 1.0 atm C) 0.6 atm D) 0.4 atm" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Using wrong value of R or forgetting units.  
In Graham's law, taking square root wrong (lighter gas has higher rate).  
Forgetting that temperature must be in Kelvin.

Study these laws with real-life examples like cooking with pressure cooker (Boyle's and Charles's) or perfume spreading in room (diffusion and Graham's). Practice many calculations – they come frequently in JAMB! Good luck!
