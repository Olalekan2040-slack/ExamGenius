
TOPIC: Chemical EquilibriumIntroductionChemical equilibrium is when a reversible reaction goes both ways at the same time and the amounts of reactants and products stop changing.
It looks like nothing is happening, but actually forward and backward reactions are still going on at the same speed – that's why we call it dynamic equilibrium.For a complete beginner: Imagine two teams playing tug-of-war. If both teams pull with exactly the same strength, the rope doesn't move – but both teams are still pulling hard! That's equilibrium.This topic is very very important in JAMB – usually 4–6 questions every year. They ask you to:Explain what happens when you change concentration, temperature, or pressure (Le Chatelier's principle)  
Calculate the equilibrium constant Kc (and sometimes Kp)  
Understand graphs showing how concentrations become constant

We will explain everything slowly with simple examples (like the Haber process for making ammonia) and many clear pictures.Here are simple diagrams showing a reversible reaction reaching equilibrium:This picture shows the basic idea of reversible reaction with forward and backward arrows, and how concentrations become constant over time.This one shows dynamic equilibrium – forward and backward reactions continue at the same rate.This graph shows how reactant decreases and product increases until they level off at equilibrium.SUBTOPIC: Reversible ReactionsObjective 1: Understand reversible reactions and dynamic equilibriumExplanation (super simple):
A reversible reaction can go both ways: reactants → products and products → reactants.
We write it with a double arrow ⇌.At first, only forward reaction happens fast.
Later, backward reaction starts and speeds up.
Eventually, forward rate = backward rate → equilibrium!Key Points:Equilibrium is dynamic – reactions never stop  
Concentrations become constant (not zero!)  
Equilibrium can be reached from either side (reactants or products)

Visual Type: graph
Visual Items:  Concentration vs time graph showing leveling off

Question 2: "At equilibrium the concentrations of reactants and products: A) Become zero B) Become constant C) Keep increasing D) Keep decreasing" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking equilibrium means reactions stop (no – they continue equally)  
Saying all reactants are used up (no – some always remain)

SUBTOPIC: Le Chatelier's PrincipleObjective 2: Apply Le Chatelier's principleExplanation (very easy):
Le Chatelier's principle says:
If you change the conditions of an equilibrium system, the system will move to oppose the change.In simple words: The equilibrium "fights back" to undo what you did!Main changes JAMB asks about:Concentration  Add more reactant → equilibrium shifts right (makes more product)  
Remove product → shifts right  
Add product → shifts left

Temperature  Increase temperature → shifts in the endothermic direction (the one that absorbs heat)  
Decrease temperature → shifts in the exothermic direction

Pressure (only for gases)  Increase pressure → shifts to side with fewer gas molecules  
Decrease pressure → shifts to side with more gas molecules

Catalyst does not change equilibrium position – it only makes equilibrium faster!Here are clear diagrams showing Le Chatelier's principle for different changes:This picture shows effect of changing concentration – adding more reactant shifts to right.This one shows temperature effect on exothermic reaction (increase T shifts left).This diagram shows pressure effect – for reaction with fewer moles on right, high pressure favors products.Here is the famous Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) – high pressure favors products because fewer moles on right.Key Points:Exothermic forward → low temperature favors products  
Endothermic forward → high temperature favors products  
More moles on left → high pressure favors right (fewer moles)

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "In the reaction N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (exothermic), to get more ammonia we should: A) Increase temperature B) Decrease pressure C) Increase pressure D) Add catalyst" (Answer: C)  
Question 2: "If we remove product from equilibrium, the system will: A) Shift left B) Shift right C) Stop D) No change" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "For endothermic reaction, increasing temperature will: A) Shift left B) Shift right C) No effect D) Stop reaction" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking catalyst shifts equilibrium (it doesn't – only speeds up)  
Forgetting to check number of gas moles for pressure effect  
Confusing which direction is exo/endo

SUBTOPIC: Equilibrium Constant (Kc, Kp)Objective 3: Calculate equilibrium constantsExplanation (step by step for beginners):
The equilibrium constant tells us how far the reaction goes toward products.Kc (concentration equilibrium constant):
Kc = [products] / [reactants] (with powers from equation coefficients)Example: For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD
Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^bKc > 1 → more products at equilibrium  
Kc < 1 → more reactants  
Kc = 1 → about equal

Kp (for gases): Same but using partial pressures (p) instead of concentrations.
Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn   where Δn = moles products – moles reactants (gases only)How to calculate (easy steps):Write balanced equation  
Put equilibrium concentrations or pressures  
Plug into formula  
Calculate!

Here are simple examples and expressions:This picture shows how to write Kc expression for a simple reaction.This graph shows how concentrations become constant – we take values at equilibrium to calculate Kc.Key Points:Only (aq) and (g) in Kc (not solids or liquids)  
Kc changes with temperature (not with concentration or pressure)  
Units depend on the reaction (sometimes no units)

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "For the reaction 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, the expression for Kc is: A) [SO₃]^2 / [SO₂]^2 [O₂] B) [SO₂]^2 [O₂] / [SO₃]^2 C) [SO₃] / [SO₂] [O₂] D) [SO₃]^2" (Answer: A)  
Question 2: "If Kc is very large, the equilibrium position lies well to the: A) Left B) Right C) Center D) No effect" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Forgetting to raise concentrations to power of coefficients  
Including solids in Kc (never!)  
Thinking Kc changes when we add more reactant (no – position shifts but Kc stays same)
