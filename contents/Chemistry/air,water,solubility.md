This illustration shows how liquid air is cooled and gases are separated based on different boiling points.SUBTOPIC: AirObjective 1: Explain composition and uses of airExplanation: Air is a mixture of gases, not a compound. The main gases are nitrogen (about 78%), oxygen (21%), and small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, and others. We separate these gases by fractional distillation of liquid air (cool air to very low temperature until it becomes liquid, then warm it slowly to collect gases at different boiling points). Air is very important: oxygen for breathing and burning, nitrogen for making fertilisers (ammonia), argon for welding, carbon dioxide for fire extinguishers and drinks.Key Points:Composition (by volume): Nitrogen 78%, Oxygen 21%, Argon 0.93%, Carbon dioxide 0.04%, others (neon, helium, etc.) very small
Separation: Fractional distillation of liquid air (nitrogen boils first at -196°C, then oxygen at -183°C)
Uses: Oxygen – respiration, welding; Nitrogen – fertilisers, food packaging; Argon – bulbs, welding
Pollution: Air pollution from smoke, cars, industries adds harmful gases like CO, SO₂, NO₂

Visual Type: diagram
Visual Items:  Pie chart of air composition

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "What is the major component of air by volume? A) Oxygen B) Nitrogen C) Carbon dioxide D) Argon" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "Which gas is separated first during fractional distillation of liquid air? A) Oxygen B) Nitrogen C) Argon D) Carbon dioxide" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "The gas used in electric bulbs to prevent filament burning is: A) Oxygen B) Nitrogen C) Argon D) Carbon dioxide" (Answer: C)

Common Mistakes:  Saying air is a compound instead of a mixture.  
Thinking oxygen is the major gas (it's nitrogen).  
Confusing the order of separation in distillation.

Objective 2: Explain air pollutionExplanation: Air pollution happens when harmful substances (from factories, cars, burning waste) enter the air and affect health and environment. Common pollutants include carbon monoxide (from incomplete burning), sulphur dioxide (from coal), and dust. We control it by using cleaner fuels, catalytic converters in cars, and planting trees.Key Points:Sources: Vehicles, industries, bush burning  
Effects: Respiratory problems, acid rain, global warming  
Control: Laws on emissions, use of unleaded petrol

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "Which gas causes acid rain? A) CO B) SO₂ C) O₂ D) N₂" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking all gases in air are pollutants.

SUBTOPIC: WaterObjective 1: Explain structure and properties of waterExplanation: Water (H₂O) is a compound with one oxygen and two hydrogen atoms joined by covalent bonds, but the molecule is bent (V-shaped) with bond angle about 104.5°. This shape and hydrogen bonding between water molecules give it special properties: high boiling point (100°C), high specific heat capacity (absorbs much heat without big temperature rise), surface tension, and ability to dissolve many substances.Here are clear diagrams of water molecule structure:chemicalbook.com

This shows the Lewis structure with lone pairs on oxygen causing the bent shape.Key Points:Formula: H₂O  
Structure: Bent molecule, polar (oxygen slightly negative, hydrogen positive)  
Properties: High boiling point (due to hydrogen bonding), universal solvent, high heat capacity, ice less dense than water

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "The bond angle in water molecule is approximately: A) 180° B) 109.5° C) 104.5° D) 120°" (Answer: C)  
Question 2: "Why does ice float on water? A) Ice is denser B) Water expands on freezing C) Hydrogen bonds break D) Ice has covalent bonds" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Saying water molecule is linear.  
Thinking ice is denser than water.

This picture shows how calcium carbonate scale builds up in hard water pipes.Objective 2: Describe hardness, treatment, and purification of waterExplanation: Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, causing soap to lather poorly and scale in kettles/pipes. We soften it by boiling (temporary hardness) or adding washing soda (permanent). Purification methods include filtration, chlorination (kills germs), distillation.Key Points:Temporary hardness: Caused by bicarbonates, removed by boiling  
Permanent hardness: Caused by sulphates/chlorides, removed by adding sodium carbonate  
Purification: Filtration removes dirt, chlorination kills bacteria

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "Hard water causes: A) Good lathering with soap B) Scale in boilers C) Low boiling point D) No effect" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking all hardness is removed by boiling (only temporary).

SUBTOPIC: SolubilityObjective 1: Describe solubility and related calculationsExplanation: Solubility is how much solute can dissolve in a solvent (usually water) at a given temperature. Solubility curves show how solubility changes with temperature: most solids increase solubility with temperature, gases decrease. Saturated solution holds maximum solute, unsaturated can dissolve more, supersaturated holds more than normal (unstable, crystals form if disturbed).Here are typical solubility curves for different salts:chemguide.co.uk

This graph shows potassium nitrate solubility rises sharply with temperature, while sodium chloride is almost flat.Here are illustrations of saturated, unsaturated, and supersaturated solutions:merchantnavydecoded.com

These pictures show: unsaturated (clear), saturated (with undissolved crystals), supersaturated (crystals form suddenly when seeded).Key Points:Factors: Temperature (most solids ↑, gases ↓), pressure (gases ↑), nature of solute/solvent  
Saturated: No more solute dissolves  
Supersaturated: More than saturated, unstable  
Calculations: Solubility = mass of solute / 100 g solvent

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "From the solubility curve, which salt's solubility increases most with temperature? A) NaCl B) KNO₃ C) Na₂SO₄ D) All same" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "A solution that can dissolve more solute is: A) Saturated B) Unsaturated C) Supersaturated D) Concentrated" (Answer: B)  
Question 3: "Gas solubility in water increases when: A) Temperature increases B) Pressure increases C) Stirring D) Volume increases" (Answer: B)

Common Mistakes:  Thinking all solubilities increase with temperature (gases decrease).  
Confusing saturated with supersaturated.
