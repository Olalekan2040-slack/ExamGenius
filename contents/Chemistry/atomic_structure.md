TOPIC: Atomic Structure and BondingIntroductionThis topic explains what an atom really looks like inside (its parts and how electrons are arranged) and how atoms join together to form compounds through different types of bonds. It is very important because almost everything in chemistry depends on understanding atoms and bonding – why substances have certain properties, how they react, and why some are solid, liquid, or gas. This older but helpful model shows electrons in fixed circular paths (orbits) around the nucleus.SUBTOPIC: Atomic StructureObjective 1: Describe atomic structure and electron configurationExplanation: An atom is the smallest part of an element that can take part in chemical reaction. It has three main particles: protons (positive charge), neutrons (no charge), and electrons (negative charge). Protons and neutrons are in the small nucleus at the centre, while electrons move around it in energy levels or shells. Atomic number tells us the number of protons (and electrons in neutral atom), mass number is protons + neutrons, and isotopes are atoms of same element with different neutrons.Key Points:Protons: positive, in nucleus, number = atomic number (Z)
Neutrons: neutral, in nucleus, number = mass number (A) - atomic number
Electrons: negative, in shells around nucleus
Electron configuration: how electrons are arranged in shells (e.g., carbon: 2,4 means 2 in first shell, 4 in second)
Isotopes: same atomic number, different mass number (e.g., carbon-12 and carbon-14)

Visual Type: diagram
Visual Items:  Diagram of atom showing nucleus and electron shells  
Table of atomic number, mass number, and isotopes for common elements

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "The atomic number of an element is 8. How many electrons are in a neutral atom of this element? A) 8 B) 16 C) 0 D) 4" (Answer: A)  
Question 2: "An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. What is its mass number? A) 11 B) 12 C) 23 D) 1" (Answer: C)  
Question 3: "The electron configuration of sodium is: A) 2,8,1 B) 2,8,2 C) 2,8,8 D) 2,1" (Answer: A)

Common Mistakes:  Confusing atomic number with mass number.  
Writing electron configuration without putting maximum electrons per shell (first shell 2, second 8, etc.).  
Thinking all atoms of same element have same mass number (forgetting isotopes).

This shows how electrons fill orbitals (s, p) with arrows for spin.Objective 2: Explain types of bonding and their propertiesExplanation: Atoms join to become stable by sharing, giving, or receiving electrons. Ionic bond is between metal and non-metal (transfer of electrons), covalent is between non-metals (sharing electrons), metallic is between metals (electrons free to move), hydrogen bond is special attraction between H and O/N/F, and van der Waals are weak attractions between all molecules.Here are diagrams comparing the different types of bonding:sciencenotes.org

Key Points:Ionic: transfer of electrons, forms ions, high melting point, conduct electricity when molten or in solution  
Covalent: sharing electrons, usually low melting point, do not conduct electricity  
Metallic: free electrons, good conductors, malleable  
Hydrogen bonding: strong attraction in water, ammonia, etc., explains high boiling point of water  
Van der Waals: weakest, between non-polar molecules, increases with size

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "Which bond involves complete transfer of electrons? A) Covalent B) Ionic C) Metallic D) Hydrogen" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "Why is sodium chloride a solid at room temperature with high melting point? A) Strong ionic bonds B) Weak covalent bonds C) Free electrons D) Hydrogen bonds" (Answer: A)  
Question 3: "Hydrogen bonding is responsible for: A) High boiling point of water B) Conductivity of metals C) Low density of ice D) Both A and C" (Answer: D)

Common Mistakes:  Saying covalent compounds conduct electricity (they usually don't).  
Forgetting that ionic compounds only conduct when dissolved or molten.  
Mixing up hydrogen bonding with covalent bonding.

SUBTOPIC: BondingObjective 3: Predict shapes of simple moleculesExplanation: The shape of a molecule depends on how many electron pairs surround the central atom (VSEPR theory – Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion). Electron pairs push each other away to be as far apart as possible. No lone pairs = linear or tetrahedral, with lone pairs = bent or pyramidal.Here are excellent diagrams showing common molecular shapes:samaterials.com

These pictures show linear (BeCl₂), trigonal planar, tetrahedral (CH₄), trigonal pyramidal (NH₃), and bent (H₂O).Key Points:2 electron pairs: linear (180°)  
3 electron pairs: trigonal planar (120°)  
4 electron pairs: tetrahedral (109.5°), but with 1 lone pair = trigonal pyramidal, 2 lone pairs = bent  
Examples: CO₂ linear, CH₄ tetrahedral, NH₃ pyramidal, H₂O bent

Example Question Pattern:  Question 1: "What is the shape of methane (CH₄) molecule? A) Linear B) Tetrahedral C) Bent D) Pyramidal" (Answer: B)  
Question 2: "Ammonia (NH₃) has a trigonal pyramidal shape because: A) 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair B) 4 bonding pairs C) 2 bonding pairs D) No lone pairs" (Answer: A)  
Question 3: "The bond angle in water molecule is approximately: A) 180° B) 120° C) 109.5° D) 104.5°" (Answer: D)

Common Mistakes:  Saying water is linear (it is bent because of 2 lone pairs).  
Forgetting to count lone pairs when predicting shape.  
Using 90° or 120° for tetrahedral shape instead of 109.5°.
